The problem

Slots exist only on paper, and anyone with a PC’s AnyDesk details can connect

At Any Time

AnyDesk connects through AnyDesk’s own servers. A PC’s ID and password work outside the slot too.

Into Anyone’s Work

They can stop another group’s training run, change its files or take over its desktop.

With No Record

Nobody can tell afterwards who connected, or when.

Today each thesis group has two slots of about 12 hours a week on a lab PC. A group has one to five students, often on several devices each.

Options considered

Of six ways to give remote access, only Headscale is ours, free and policy-driven

OptionWho runs itFit for slot-based access
AnyDesk only (today)AnyDesk’s serversNo enforcement, no record of who connected
SSH forwarding or bastionPorts opened on campusSSH exposed to the internet, slots enforced on each PC
Plain WireGuard hubUs, all traffic through the hubNo NAT traversal, keys and rules by hand per device
Tailscale (hosted)Tailscale Inc., off campusFree for 6 users and $8 per user a month after that. Data stays off campus
NetBird (self-hosted)UsFive services to run, and its own clients
Headscale (chosen)Us, direct peer-to-peerOne program and SQLite, official Tailscale apps, free (BSD-3)

The deciding feature is that the whole access policy is one document, rewritten and loaded at every slot change.

Tailscale pricing from tailscale.com/pricing, October 2026

4

We compared the realistic options. Port forwarding exposes SSH to the internet. Plain WireGuard means managing keys by hand and pushing everything through one hub. Hosted Tailscale works well but costs money per user beyond six. It also keeps the control and user data off campus. NetBird is capable but heavier to run. Headscale is one program with SQLite and uses the official Tailscale apps. It is free, and its access policy is one document we regenerate at every slot change. That last point is why we chose it.

How it works · a connection

A connection goes direct when it can, and through our own relay when it cannot

STEP 1

Sign In and Get Peers

Each device keeps an HTTPS session to Headscale and gets only the peers the policy allows.

STEP 2

Start on Our Relay

A new connection starts through our relay (DERP) while STUN finds each end’s public address.

STEP 3

Go Direct if Possible

If a direct UDP path works, traffic moves to it. If UDP is blocked, it stays relayed over TCP 443.

ALWAYS

Encrypted End to End

Every path is WireGuard. The relay only ever sees ciphertext, and Headscale never sees traffic.

Our relay runs on the lab server, so relayed traffic stays on campus.

Off campus with only TCP 443 open, the rehearsal still connected at 27–37 ms.

Based on the Tailscale post on NAT traversal (2020)

7

This is how a student's laptop reaches the PC. The device signs in and learns which peers it may talk to. The connection starts through our own relay, so it works at once. Meanwhile both ends try to punch a direct path through their NATs. If that works, traffic moves to the direct path. On networks that block UDP it stays on the relay over port 443, slower but working. Either way it is encrypted end to end with WireGuard. The relay is on our server, so nothing goes through a third party.

Admins keep doing what they do today and put a group's code in the Sheet. The website reads the Sheet every 30 seconds and checks every cell. A bad cell keeps its last good value and is reported. The slot agent runs every 15 seconds. Slots run from 06:00 to 17:55 and from 18:00 to 05:55. At 17:55 the slot closes first. The policy stops letting the outgoing group in, and its sessions and processes end on each PC. For five minutes only the admins can reach the PC. No policy at all would mean open access, so a failure always means closed.

Each group logs in as grp-<code> on its PC · Example policy in Appendix C

9

This is the access model the policy encodes. Admins can reach every PC. The slot agent can reach every PC too, but it can only log in as its own service account, labsched. That account can do one thing, which is to switch the active account. A group in its slot reaches its own PC and logs in as its group account. Everyone else reaches nothing, including the same group outside its slot. Logins use Tailscale SSH, which checks the caller's tailnet identity. There are no SSH keys or passwords to hand out or revoke.

Every failure ends in no access rather than open access. If the policy cannot be built, only the admins get in. If Google or the website is unreachable, the agent keeps enforcing its last copy. Appendix A · devices

A 15-PC pilot means 91 to 916 devices, well inside what we tested

A device is not a user, so a student with a laptop and a phone is two devices. Devices stay registered, and access follows the policy rather than the device list.

A day after a student leaves every group, their devices are removed. An optional switch also removes extra devices, and it starts as a dry run.

Stress test held 871 devices on 4 cores (slide 18)

25

This answers a question about scale. The Headscale FAQ is cautious and says it can likely handle hundreds of devices. Our worst case for the pilot is about 900 devices, with every student keeping two devices all week. The stress test held 871 on 4 cores. We therefore keep devices registered rather than removing them every slot. Removing them would force students to sign in again before each slot.

Appendix B · remote desktop

AnyDesk goes around the policy, so remote desktop moves onto the tailnet

AnyDesk Today

Connects through AnyDesk’s own servers, not our tailnet, so our policy cannot control it. Its free license does not cover lab use.

Planned on the Tailnet

Unattended AnyDesk turned off on enrolled PCs. RustDesk in direct mode, or xrdp, on a port added to the group’s slot.

Status: Designed, not built or tested yet. The same policy will then control remote desktop exactly as it controls SSH.

Appendix C · the policy

The policy is rewritten at every slot change, as here for Monday 06:00

"groups": { "group:thesis-a": ["s2101@g.bracu.ac.bd", "s2102@g.bracu.ac.bd", …] },

"grants": [

  { "src": ["group:admins"],   "dst": ["tag:pc-01", …], "ip": ["*"] },

  { "src": ["tag:scheduler"],  "dst": ["tag:pc-01", …], "ip": ["tcp:22"] },

  // this slot, Mon 06:00–17:55: pc-01 shared with Group A

  { "src": ["group:thesis-a"], "dst": ["tag:pc-01"],    "ip": ["tcp:22"] } ],

"ssh": [

  { "action": "accept", "src": ["group:thesis-a"], "dst": ["tag:pc-01"],    "users": ["grp-thesis-a"] },

  { "action": "accept", "src": ["tag:scheduler"],  "dst": ["tag:pc-01", …], "users": ["labsched"] } ]

The orange lines are the only part that changes between slots. If the policy cannot be built, the agent loads the admins-only version instead.
