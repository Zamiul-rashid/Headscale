\textbf{Summary:} Every thesis group already has booked slots on our lab PCs,
but nothing enforces them. Groups work remotely through AnyDesk. Anyone with a
PC's AnyDesk details can connect at any time. We have built and tested a system
that enforces the slots. A self-hosted Headscale server admits only students
who sign in with their BRACU Google account. Headscale is an open-source control
server for the Tailscale and WireGuard VPN. The lab's weekly routine stays in a
Google Sheet. Our slot system, labslots, reads it every 30 seconds.

At each slot change, labslots rewrites the network policy and switches the PC to
the incoming group's account. Only the group in its slot can then reach its PC.
Every login to a lab PC uses Tailscale SSH, so there are no keys or passwords to
hand out or take back. The system already runs on our own hardware with three
lab PCs. In a test with 871 simulated devices on a 4-core server, every slot
change took effect within 20 seconds. None of 2,956 login checks got in where it
should not have. A 15-PC pilot on campus needs one public IP address, one domain
name under \code{bracu.ac.bd}, two inbound ports and a lab email account
(Section~\ref{sec:uni}). A third port for HTTP is optional.

\section{Background and Goals}

Each thesis group is assigned two slots of just under 12 hours per week on a
lab PC. Groups work remotely through AnyDesk, and the slots exist only on paper.
AnyDesk connects through its own servers. Anyone who knows a PC's ID and
password can therefore connect whenever they like. That person can stop another
group's training run, change its files or take over its desktop. Nobody can tell
afterwards who did it. A group has one to five students, and students often use
more than one device. Physical access stays under the lab's existing rules. The
pilot has seven aims.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em]
  \item Enforce the slots. During a slot only that group can reach its PC
        remotely, and outside it nobody except the lab admins can.
  \item Run Headscale and the slot system on one machine in the lab, reachable
        through one public IP address and one domain name.
  \item Enroll 15 Linux lab PCs, each registered once as a lab-owned machine.
  \item Let students sign in with their BRACU Google account, so the lab keeps
        no passwords.
  \item Switch access automatically at every slot change, and record every
        change and every enforcement step.
  \item Keep the schedule in the Google Sheet the lab already uses, and take
        group enrollments through our own page instead of a Google Form.
  \item Measure reliability and load before any wider rollout.
\end{enumerate}

\section{System Architecture}

The design separates a control plane from a data plane (Figure~\ref{fig:arch}).
Headscale is an open-source reimplementation of Tailscale's coordination server
that works with the official Tailscale clients. It registers devices and hands
out tailnet addresses and keys. It also pushes each device a peer list filtered
by the access policy. User traffic never passes through it. The data plane is
WireGuard, running directly between the student's laptop and the lab PC. Next
to Headscale runs labslots, which is a website (the \emph{hub}) and a \emph{slot
agent} that enforces the schedule.

\textbf{How a Connection Is Made:} Each connection passes through four stages.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em]
  \item Every device keeps an HTTPS (TCP 443) session to Headscale and
        receives only the peers the policy allows.
  \item A new connection starts out relayed through a DERP server. Meanwhile
        both ends learn their public addresses through STUN (UDP 3478) and attempt
        NAT traversal.
  \item If a direct UDP path works, traffic moves to it for the lowest latency.
        Otherwise traffic stays on DERP over TCP 443. That path is slower but
        works, and the client keeps checking for a direct path under hard NAT or
        blocked UDP.
  \item Every path is encrypted end to end with WireGuard. The relay only ever
        sees ciphertext.
\end{enumerate}

Our host runs the embedded DERP relay of Headscale, so relayed traffic stays on
campus. Tailscale's public DERP servers can stay in the map as a backup, or be
removed to keep everything in house.

\textbf{One Entry Point:} Caddy terminates TLS for \code{hs.cse.bracu.ac.bd} with
an automatic Let's Encrypt certificate. It sends \code{/slots} to the labslots
website and everything else to Headscale. Headscale needs WebSocket support from
the proxy, which Caddy provides. Our current deployment already runs behind a
reverse proxy. STUN (UDP 3478) goes straight to Headscale. Lab PCs reach the
server on its campus LAN address through a hosts-file entry. They therefore do
not depend on the campus firewall looping traffic back to the public IP (hairpin
NAT).

\subsection{Machines, Users and Slots}

Each lab PC is registered once with a pre-authorized key that carries its tag
(\code{tag:pc-NN}). That makes it a \emph{tagged node}, owned by the lab rather
than by a person. Every student is a user in our tailnet, identified by their
BRACU email. A thesis group is a list of emails in the access policy and one
Linux account on its PC (\code{grp-<code>}). To share a PC with a group, the
policy holds a grant that exists only during the group's slot. The PC cannot
start connections back to the students.

Each PC has 14 slots per week, 06:00--17:55 and 18:00--05:55 every day. A slot
ends five minutes before the next one starts, and that switch-over belongs to no
group. A group normally gets two slots on different days (Table~\ref{tab:slots}).
The routine is kept in a Google Sheet with one \emph{Routine} tab per term. That
tab has a row per PC and a column per slot, and an admin types a group's code
into a cell. Other tabs hold the groups, PCs, terms and admins. The labslots
website reads the Sheet every 30 seconds and checks every cell. A bad cell keeps
its last good value and is listed in the \emph{Problems} tab of the Sheet. If
Google cannot be reached, the system keeps enforcing its last good copy.

The slot agent runs next to Headscale and acts every 15 seconds. A missed
boundary, such as a restart or an outage, is therefore put right on the next
run. At the end of a slot the agent closes the slot first. It lets the next
group in only when that slot starts, five minutes later. Each run has three
steps.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em]
  \item The agent writes the policy for the current slot and loads it through
        Headscale's API. In the switch-over the policy grants no group. All 15
        PCs change in one update. Network access and Tailscale SSH logins change
        here, and each PC ends the outgoing group's open sessions itself.
  \item The agent logs in to each PC as the scheduler node over Tailscale SSH and
        runs \code{slot-switch}. The helper stops the outgoing group's processes.
        Once the next slot starts, it makes the incoming group's account the only
        active one.
  \item On any error, the agent loads an admins-only policy, so access fails
        closed. Having no policy at all would allow everything.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Lab PCs:} Each PC runs Ubuntu LTS with the Tailscale client and
Tailscale SSH turned on. On the tailnet, port 22 is answered by the Tailscale
client itself. It checks the caller's tailnet identity against the \code{ssh}
rules of the policy. A group member logs in as the group's account and the
scheduler only as \code{labsched}. Admins log in as any non-root account. Nobody
holds an SSH key, and there is nothing to revoke when a student leaves. One setup
script per PC turns Tailscale SSH on and installs the \code{slot-switch} helper.
Home directories persist between slots, but running jobs stop at slot end.
Groups must therefore checkpoint their work.

\subsection{Student Login and Enrollment}

Headscale does not check passwords itself. It hands sign-in to Google over OIDC
and admits only verified accounts on BRACU's domains. The labslots website signs
people in the same way. The lab stores no passwords, and a student who forgets
theirs uses Google's own recovery.

\textbf{What the Student Does:} The process has four steps.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em]
  \item One member of the group signs in to the enrollment page with their BRACU
        Google account. The member sends the group name, the thesis title, and
        the supervisor and any co-supervisors with their emails. The form also
        lists one to five members, each with name, student ID and BRACU G Suite
        address. Personal addresses are refused. This page replaces the Google
        Form the lab used before.
  \item An admin reviews the enrollment and assigns a PC and weekly slots in one
        step. Both are written into the Sheet. The group sees its PC, its slots
        and how to connect on its status page. Every member is emailed once
        outgoing email is set up.
  \item Each member runs \code{tailscale login -{}-login-server}
        \code{https://hs.cse.bracu.ac.bd} once, or presses \emph{Log in} in the
        Tailscale app. They then sign in with their BRACU account.
  \item During a slot, a member connects with \code{ssh grp-a@lab-pc-01}.
        Outside the slot, the PC cannot be reached.
\end{enumerate}
\emph{Sign-in proves who a student is, and the schedule decides what that student
can reach.} Students can keep a personal Tailscale account alongside ours,
because client 1.34 and later supports several accounts.

\textbf{The Admin Desk:} Admins use the same website. It shows the PCs in use now
and what changes next, a calendar of the routine and the enrollment queue. It
also shows the state of each PC and the enforcement settings with a policy
preview. A log records every change and every enforcement step.

Headscale's FAQ says it can likely handle hundreds of devices when the network
changes rarely. Every change rebuilds the network map for every node. For that
reason our earlier plan removed every device at the end of its slot, so students
would sign in again before each slot. Our stress test (Section~\ref{sec:tests})
shows this is not needed at pilot scale. A 4-core server held 871 devices that
never left and stayed almost idle between slot changes. Devices therefore
stay registered, and access follows the policy rather than the device list.
Clean-up removes only what is no longer needed.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.6em]
  \item A day after a student leaves every group, their devices are removed.
        This happens when their group or term is removed from the Sheet.
  \item An optional switch also removes devices outside their slot and beyond
        two per student. It starts in dry-run mode and lists what it would
        remove.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Remote Desktop in the Slot (Planned)}

Groups work through AnyDesk today, and that is where the slots are bypassed.
AnyDesk connects through its own servers rather than our tailnet, so our policy
cannot control it. Its free license also does not cover lab use. We therefore
plan to turn off unattended AnyDesk access on enrolled PCs. A remote desktop
that runs over the tailnet replaces it, either RustDesk in direct-IP mode or
xrdp. Its port is added to the ports a group gets in its slot, so the same
policy controls it as it controls SSH. This part is not built or tested yet.

\textbf{Nothing Else Is Exposed:} The lab PCs, port 22 and the services behind
Caddy are not reachable from the internet. Student traffic reaches a PC only
through WireGuard, and only during the group's slot.

normally allow this already. If outbound UDP is blocked, connections still work
through our relay on TCP 443, only more slowly.
